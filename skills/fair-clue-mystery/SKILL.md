- Decide the solution first: who, how, why, and the one fact that gives it away. Then plan backwards.
- Every clue the solution needs is shown to the reader before the reveal, at least two chapters before, and shown as plainly as to the investigator.
- Hide clues by placement, not by withholding: put a clue in a list of ordinary things, at a moment of distraction, or give it a second, innocent explanation.
- Red herrings must be true things that point the wrong way, never lies by the narrator. On a reread they should still hold.
- No solution may rest on a twin, a secret passage, an unknown poison, a stranger never met, or a coincidence.
- The investigator's reasoning at the reveal uses only what the reader has seen. Let the reveal reorder known facts rather than add new ones.
- Each chapter should change the list of suspects or the reader's theory: someone is cleared, someone is newly suspected, a timeline shifts.
