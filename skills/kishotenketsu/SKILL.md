- The book moves in four parts. Ki: introduce people and place. Shō: develop them, deepen what we know, without a big reversal. Ten: a turn, often something unexpected or apparently unrelated, that changes how everything before it is seen. Ketsu: bring the threads together so the new view settles into meaning.
- Tension comes from contrast and anticipation, not necessarily from an antagonist. Two things placed side by side make the reader wonder how they connect.
- Do not invent a villain to supply conflict. Characters can still want things and meet obstacles, but the story's energy is in understanding, not in winning.
- Plan the turn first: what is the fact, encounter or perspective that, once known, makes the first half mean something else? Plant the ground for it so it feels inevitable afterwards.
- The ending reconciles rather than resolves: an image or a choice that holds both halves together.
- Spread the four parts over the planned chapters, roughly a quarter each, with the turn near three quarters.
