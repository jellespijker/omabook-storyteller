- The reader is a collaborator. The pleasure of reading is in reconstructing feelings from behaviour, inferring motives from actions, holding tension across scenes and guessing what comes next. Prose that explains, resolves and names everything does that work for them and takes the pleasure away.
- So: let a feeling show in what a person does, notices or avoids, and do not name it as well, not even in passing ("he had been glad", "calmer than usual", "a little hurt"). A named feeling is the reader's work done for them. Let a line of dialogue mean more than it says, and do not explain what it meant. Leave a question open across a scene break.
- Trust is not obscurity. The reader still needs to know where they are, whose head they are in, and what that person wants. Orient them plainly; leave the meaning to them.
- Keep to what the viewpoint character could know at this moment. You know the whole plan; they know only what they have lived through. Write from inside their knowledge, including what they would miss.
- Make each element do two jobs: a line of dialogue that moves the plot and shows character, a detail that grounds the scene and shows who is looking, a transition that skips time and carries a mood. Something that does only one job, and that the scene would not miss, can go.
- Ask of every explanation: would the reader lose anything if it were cut? If they could have worked it out, cut it. Watch the end of a scene above all: the sentence where someone "understood that…" or "realised what she meant" hands the reader the answer they were about to find. End on the line or the gesture, and leave the understanding to them.
- Check which pleasure a flat passage is failing: being in the world (grounding, steady viewpoint), the sentences themselves (rhythm, word choice), the people (distinct voices, feelings to read), the flow (pace that fits), or curiosity (an open question, a setup waiting for its payoff).
